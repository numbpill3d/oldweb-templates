/// DEEP DIVE — ISSUE 07.03 FEATURE
THE MAGAZINE AS A
DELIVERY SYSTEM
FOR DANGEROUS IDEAS

Before the algorithm, there was the editor. Before the feed, there was the layout. Before the engagement metric, there was the cover price — and the desperate, almost religious act of paying actual money to hold ideas in your hands on glossy, ink-soaked paper.

The tech magazines of the early-to-mid 1990s were not merely publications. They were polemics. They were dispatches from a war nobody had officially declared. Wired launched in 1993 with the energy of a manifesto and the production values of a Rem Koolhaas fever dream.

Designer John Plunkett and art director Barbara Kuhr built pages that hurt. Cyan on magenta. Text reversed out of photographs. Sidebar copy at 7 points. Pull quotes that jumped grids and ran diagonally. You could not read Wired passively. The medium demanded engagement.

Mondo 2000 was even more unhinged, in the best possible way. R.U. Sirius and Queen Mu ran a publication that looked like it had been assembled at 3am after a long night of interesting conversations. Articles about smart drugs sat next to interviews with William Gibson sat next to classified ads for brain machines.

"Information density is not just a design choice. It is an editorial philosophy. The page that fights back is the page worth reading."

2600 represented a third mode entirely — the utilitarian screed. Cheap newsprint. No art direction to speak of. But inside: the actual technical details that the establishment wanted to suppress.

What united all three was a shared belief: that information, freely distributed and densely packed, was inherently political. The layout was not decoration. The layout was the argument.

/// COLUMN 01 — HARDWARE
SILICON GOSSIP

Intel has begun sampling its P6 to select OEMs and the benchmark numbers are, frankly, obscene. 200MHz with a new 256KB L2 cache architecture that sits on-die rather than on the motherboard. The old P5 bus bottleneck is dead. Whether this translates to actual real-world performance gains for anyone running Quake is another question entirely.

Meanwhile, the clone market is heating up. AMD's K6 looks credible for the first time in years. Cyrix is making noise about the M2. The days of Intel's monopoly on raw performance might, genuinely, be numbered.

But the real story is storage. The 4GB IDE drive has cracked the consumer price ceiling. $199 retail. Within 18 months the 10GB mark will fall and nobody will remember what "zipping to floppy" means.

/// COLUMN 02 — CULTURE
SIGNAL / NOISE

There is a peculiar class of person who believes that the correct response to "the internet" is to write a thinkpiece about it in a magazine. You are currently reading one. We acknowledge the irony without resolving it.

What is interesting is not the content of the internet — content has always been cheap, disposable, abundant. What is interesting is the protocol. TCP/IP is not a technology. It is a philosophy of distribution made executable. Every packet routed around censorship is a political act of the purest possible kind.

The culture writers who cover the net as a lifestyle story are missing the point in the most spectacular possible way. The net is not a lifestyle. The net is infrastructure. And infrastructure is always, eventually, political.

/// COLUMN 03 — SOFTWARE
CODE REVIEW

Netscape 4.0 is a bloated mess and Navigator is losing the war to Internet Explorer through a combination of Microsoft's licensing stranglehold on OEM hardware and the fact that IE 3.0 is genuinely not bad anymore. The browser wars are real. The casualties are standards compliance and anything approaching a coherent DOM.

JavaScript is six months old and already being abused in ways that Brendan Eich almost certainly did not intend when he wrote it in ten days in 1995. The web is becoming programmable. This is either the best or worst thing that has happened to it. Possibly both.

Meanwhile, Linux 2.0 quietly runs on everything. The kernel mailing list has more commits this month than all of Microsoft's public repositories combined. Open source is not a business model. It is a force of nature.

WIRED MAGAZINE
VOLUME 1, ISSUE 1 — THE BIRTH DOCUMENT
MARCH/APRIL 1993  ·  SAN FRANCISCO, CA  ·  100K PRINT RUN
Manifesto disguised as a magazine. Mitch Kapor on the electronic frontier. Bruce Sterling on the shape of things to come. A design language that looked like nothing before it — fluorescent colors on coated stock, typography that treated the grid as a suggestion. The opening editorial claimed it was "for the people who created the digital revolution and for the people who are trying to adapt to it, survive it, and — if possible — control it."

MONDO 2000
ISSUE 7 — SMART DRUGS & STEERING THE EVOLUTION
SUMMER 1991  ·  BERKELEY, CA  ·  40K ESTIMATED CIRCULATION
Perhaps the strangest thing ever sold on newsstands. An interview with Timothy Leary sits adjacent to a technical piece on neural network theory. R.U. Sirius writes about the coming democratization of psychopharmacology. Queen Mu's editorial introduces the concept of the "cyberpunk lifestyle" — not as fiction, but as actionable philosophy. The typography was even more chaotic than Wired's and the paper quality even worse. The ideas were incandescent.

2600: THE HACKER QUARTERLY
SPRING 1993 — SYSTEMS & SUBVERSIONS
Q1 1993  ·  MIDDLE ISLAND, NY  ·  NEWSPRINT, 48PP
Emmanuel Goldstein's quarterly bible of the phone phreaking and early hacking community. No art direction, no photography, no production values beyond the absolute functional minimum. And yet: arguably more important than Wired for the communities it served. Technical documentation for telephone system exploitation, bulletin board system directories, legal defense fund updates from arrested hackers, and the classified personals that served as pre-internet networking for people who needed to stay off the grid.
PHREAKING UNDERGROUND GOLDSTEIN
WIRED MAGAZINE
3.12 — THE ENCYCLOPAEDIA OF THE NEW ECONOMY
DECEMBER 1996  ·  SPECIAL ISSUE  ·  280PP
By 1996, Wired had codified its own vocabulary. This A-to-Z special issue defined the terms of the new digital economy — from "bandwidth" to "disintermediation" to "meme" (a word most readers encountered here for the first time, borrowed from Dawkins and redeployed for the internet age). Kevin Kelly's introductory essay argued that the new rules of the networked economy would overturn all previous models of value creation. He was approximately half right about everything, which in retrospect looks like prescience.

WHY WE PRINT
WHAT WE PRINT

We produce this archive as a counter-argument to forgetting. The internet — the same internet that these magazines heralded, celebrated, and occasionally overhyped — has largely erased the print record of its own origin story.

You cannot Google your way into understanding what it felt like to hold Wired Issue 1 in 1993, or to find a copy of Mondo 2000 in a San Francisco bookstore in 1991 and feel, for the first time, that there were other people who thought about the world the way you did.

Print had density. Not just information density — though god knows it had that — but physical density. Weight. Smell. A tactile argument that the ideas inside were worth pressing into dead trees and shipping across the country.

What we are doing here is archaeology. We are brushing dirt off artifacts. We believe the artifacts matter — not as nostalgia, but as evidence. Evidence that ideas had urgency. That design could be political. That a magazine could be a weapon.

BEING DIGITAL — NICHOLAS NEGROPONTE
KNOPF, 1995  ·  NONFICTION  ·  256PP
9.1
The MIT Media Lab founder's crystallization of everything the Wired community believed in 1995. Prophetic about the shift from atoms to bits in ways that make uncomfortable reading now that we know which corporation collected all the bits. Essential primary source for understanding the idealism of the era.
NEUROMANCER — WILLIAM GIBSON
ACE BOOKS, 1984  ·  FICTION  ·  271PP
10.0
Not a review. An acknowledgment. Gibson named cyberspace in 1984 before most people who would eventually live in it had heard of modems. The debt every magazine on this site owes to this book is incalculable and was acknowledged, constantly, by every editor involved.
THE VIRTUAL COMMUNITY — HOWARD RHEINGOLD
ADDISON-WESLEY, 1993  ·  NONFICTION  ·  325PP
8.4
Rheingold's account of the WELL and the early online communities that preceded the web proper. Pre-internet social networks, pre-social media community drama, pre-everything we now accept as normal. Both more utopian and more honest than anything published since.
OUT OF CONTROL — KEVIN KELLY
ADDISON-WESLEY, 1994  ·  NONFICTION  ·  521PP
8.8
Kelly's massive synthesis of evolutionary theory, network science, and cyberculture. The founding executive editor of Wired wrote this as a kind of grand unified theory of the digital age. 500 pages of dense, generous, occasionally wrong, always interesting thinking about emergence, complexity, and why the future will be grown rather than built.
